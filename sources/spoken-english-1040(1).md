# English You Can Actually Say

**1,040 speaking items • 26 numbered parts • English–Arabic**

For an approximately B1 learner who wants to tell stories, express feelings, explain problems, make plans, and keep everyday conversations going.

## How to read the collection

- Each part has 40 entries. The numbers are study IDs, not frequency ranks.
- Arabic gives the meaning used in the example. It uses clear everyday Arabic, with familiar Egyptian phrasing where helpful. It is not an exhaustive dictionary translation.
- Learn the phrase and its sentence pattern together. Change the people, time, place, and reason to build your own sentences.
- The collection combines intermediate vocabulary with familiar words in useful combinations. An easy root can form a valuable speaking phrase: “get the wrong idea” is more useful here than a bare entry for “get.”
- Some entries are common stretch items. “Stretch” flags a potentially harder item; it is not a formally verified CEFR rating. BrE means British English; AmE means American English.
- The Part of Speech column sometimes identifies a functional unit, such as a sentence frame or discourse marker, rather than a single grammatical word class.
- A blank usage note means the example needs no additional short explanation. Many expressions have other meanings not taught in that row.

## A short daily speaking routine

Choose **8 items**, not a whole part. Read their examples aloud. Hide the Arabic and explain the meanings. Make one true sentence about your own life for each item. Then record a 60–90 second answer using at least four of them. Listen once and try again with fewer pauses. Review after one day, three days, and a week. This is a suggested practice routine, not a research-tested schedule for this particular resource.

For frames such as “I'd rather,” change the ending: “I'd rather stay home,” “I'd rather take the train,” “I'd rather talk tomorrow.” For ordinary vocabulary, keep a useful partner: “heavy workload,” “meet a deadline,” “feel relieved.” Aim to recall and use phrases without reading them.

## Research and selection

The organizing principle is **communicative usefulness**, informed by spoken-language research rather than alphabetical order. McCarthy and Carter discuss the role of frequent conversational combinations and markers [S1]. Spoken BNC2014 contains recorded everyday conversation [S2]. Oxford's frequency-and-relevance approach and representative CEFR checks helped calibrate difficulty [S3–S4]. PHaVE research and its UC Davis explanation supported attention to frequent phrasal-verb meanings [S5–S6].

Selection favoured reusable language for real speaking tasks. Bare beginner objects, specialist terminology, literary language, and unnecessary formal synonyms were avoided. Original conversational examples and contextual Arabic meanings were written for this collection. Closely related entries remain when they teach different functions, patterns, or useful distinctions—for example, “on time” versus “in time,” and “used to” versus “be used to.”

**Verification scope:** published corpus findings and source materials were examined, and representative vocabulary levels were checked. This is an editorially curated study collection, not an extraction of the top 1,040 corpus entries. No live concordance or individual frequency check was performed for every item. Oxford's general corpus is not exclusively spoken; learner lists are not themselves evidence that every phrase is frequent in casual speech. Some public dictionary and repository pages were blocked, so the relevant checks used their indexed results. Examples and Arabic meanings are original, not quotations from those sources.

Research checked: **1 October 2026**. The collection is designed around B1 speaking needs, with useful stretch vocabulary. It does not claim that every word, meaning, or phrase has an official B1 label.
